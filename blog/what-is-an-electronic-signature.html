What Is an Electronic Signature? Everything You Need to Know

SS
Signature Sketch Team
Published: March 4, 2026
7 min read

You just received a contract in your inbox. The other party wants it signed by end of day. You do not own a printer. Sound familiar? This is the exact situation electronic signatures were designed to solve — and they have been legally valid for over two decades.

The Short Answer

An electronic signature is any electronic indication that a person agrees to the contents of a document. That is the legal definition, and it is intentionally broad. A typed name at the bottom of an email counts. So does a checkbox on a web form, a finger-drawn squiggle on a touchscreen, or a signature image inserted into a PDF.

The critical element is not what the signature looks like — it is intent. If you applied a mark electronically with the purpose of signing, it qualifies as an electronic signature. This distinction matters because it means you do not need fancy software, cryptographic certificates, or a specific device. You just need to demonstrate that you meant to sign.

How Electronic Signatures Actually Work

The mechanics are simpler than most people expect. When you sign a document electronically, the platform (or you, manually) captures several pieces of information:

  • The signature itself — whether drawn, typed, or uploaded as an image
  • A timestamp recording exactly when the signature was applied
  • The signer's email address or identity information
  • An IP address showing where the signing took place
  • Sometimes a hash of the document, proving it has not been altered since signing

This collection of data is called an audit trail, and it is actually more robust than a traditional pen-on-paper signature. With a wet signature, all you have is a mark on a page. With an electronic signature, you have a timestamped, traceable record of who signed, when, and from where.

For everyday business documents, the process is straightforward: you create a signature (by drawing it online or typing your name in a cursive font), save it as a transparent PNG, and insert it into your document. Done.

Are Electronic Signatures Actually Legal?

This is the question everyone asks first, and the answer is unambiguous: yes. Electronic signatures carry the same legal weight as handwritten signatures in virtually every major economy on the planet.

In the United States, two laws establish this: the ESIGN Act (Electronic Signatures in Global and National Commerce Act, signed into law on June 30, 2000) and the UETA (Uniform Electronic Transactions Act, adopted by 49 states). Together, they state that a contract or signature cannot be denied legal effect solely because it is in electronic form.

In the European Union, the eIDAS Regulation (effective since July 2016) provides a unified legal framework across all 27 member states. The UK has its own Electronic Communications Act. Canada, Australia, India, Japan, Brazil, and South Korea all have equivalent legislation.

The exceptions are narrow

Wills and testaments, certain real estate deeds requiring notarization, family court orders (adoption, divorce), and some government-specific forms may still require a physical signature depending on your jurisdiction. But for standard business contracts, NDAs, invoices, employment agreements, and purchase orders? Electronic signatures are fully enforceable everywhere.

Three Levels of Electronic Signatures

Not all electronic signatures offer the same level of security. The EU's eIDAS regulation defines three tiers, and this framework has become the global reference point even outside Europe:

Simple Electronic Signature (SES)

The broadest category. A typed name, a scanned signature image, a checkbox, or a signature drawn with a free online generator all qualify. No identity verification beyond what the platform provides. This is what 90% of businesses use for everyday documents, and it is perfectly legal.

Advanced Electronic Signature (AES)

A step up in security. An AES must be uniquely linked to the signer, capable of identifying them, created using data under their sole control, and linked to the document so any subsequent change is detectable. Think two-factor authentication plus a secure signing environment. Used for higher-value transactions where extra assurance is needed.

Qualified Electronic Signature (QES)

The gold standard. A QES is an advanced electronic signature created by a qualified device and backed by a certificate from a government-approved authority. Under eIDAS, it has the exact same legal standing as a handwritten signature in all EU member states — no questions asked. Required only for specific regulated transactions like certain government filings.

For the vast majority of business use cases — signing a freelance contract, approving an invoice, onboarding a new employee — a simple electronic signature is all you need.

Electronic Signature vs Digital Signature

These two terms get mixed up constantly, but they refer to different things. Understanding the distinction saves confusion down the road.

Aspect Electronic Signature Digital Signature
What it is Any electronic mark showing agreement A cryptographic proof of identity
Technology required None — a typed name works PKI encryption, certificates
Tamper detection Not built-in Yes — any change invalidates it
Cost Free Usually requires paid certificates
Common use Contracts, NDAs, HR docs Government filings, software signing

The simplest way to think about it: all digital signatures are electronic signatures, but most electronic signatures are not digital signatures. A digital signature is a specific technical implementation using cryptographic algorithms. An electronic signature is the broader legal concept.

Unless you are filing documents with a government agency or working in a heavily regulated industry, you almost certainly need an electronic signature, not a digital one.

Five Things People Get Wrong About E-Signatures

After years of helping people create signatures online, we have seen the same misconceptions come up repeatedly. Let us clear them up.

Myth 1: "E-signatures are not as valid as real signatures"

They are equally valid under the law. The ESIGN Act explicitly states that a signature cannot be denied legal effect solely because it is electronic. Courts have upheld this consistently for over 25 years.

Myth 2: "You need special software"

You need a browser and about 30 seconds. Paid platforms like DocuSign add workflow features (sending documents for signature, tracking status, reminders), but the signature itself can be created with any free tool. The legal validity comes from intent and consent, not from the software.

Myth 3: "An e-signature has to look like your handwriting"

Legally, no. A typed name, a checkbox, or even an "I Accept" button click qualifies. That said, a handwritten-style signature carries more perceived authority in business contexts. It signals that you took the time to sign personally rather than just clicking a button.

Myth 4: "The other party has to use the same platform"

Not at all. You can create your signature with one tool and the other party can use a completely different method. What matters is that both parties intended to sign and consented to the electronic process. The signature image itself is just a PNG file — it works everywhere.

Myth 5: "E-signatures are easy to forge"

Actually, electronic signatures are harder to forge than handwritten ones. A wet signature can be traced or copied from any document. An electronic signature backed by an audit trail (timestamp, IP address, email verification) provides far more evidence of authenticity than ink on paper ever could.

Where Can You Use Electronic Signatures?

The better question might be: where can you not use them? The list of accepted use cases is enormous:

  • Business contracts — service agreements, vendor contracts, partnership deals
  • Employment documents — offer letters, NDAs, non-competes, onboarding forms
  • Financial documents — invoices, purchase orders, expense approvals
  • Real estate — lease agreements, rental applications (deeds may vary by state)
  • Healthcare — patient consent forms, HIPAA authorizations, insurance claims
  • Sales — proposals, quotes, order confirmations
  • Internal approvals — budget sign-offs, policy acknowledgments, time sheets

If you are unsure whether a specific document type accepts electronic signatures in your jurisdiction, check with a legal professional. But for standard commercial transactions, you are covered.

Tips for Using Electronic Signatures Professionally

Creating the signature is the easy part. Using it well requires a bit of thought:

  • Save your signature file somewhere accessible. Cloud storage, your phone's photo library, or a dedicated folder on your desktop. You will use it more often than you think.
  • Use transparent PNG format. A signature with a white background will cover text in your documents. Transparent backgrounds let the signature sit naturally on any page.
  • Keep it consistent. Use the same signature across all your documents. Switching between different signatures can raise questions about authenticity.
  • Match the formality to the document. A hand-drawn signature feels more personal for contracts and legal documents. A typed signature in a professional font works well for emails and internal memos.
  • Always keep a signed copy. After signing, save the completed document as a PDF. This preserves the signature and creates your record of the agreement.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is an electronic signature legally binding?

Yes. The ESIGN Act (US), eIDAS (EU), and equivalent laws in the UK, Canada, Australia, and dozens of other countries give electronic signatures the same legal standing as handwritten ones for most commercial and business documents.

What is the difference between an electronic signature and a digital signature?

An electronic signature is any electronic mark indicating agreement. A digital signature is a specific type that uses cryptographic encryption for identity verification and tamper detection. For most business documents, a simple electronic signature is sufficient.

Can I create an electronic signature for free?

Absolutely. Signature Sketch lets you draw or type a signature in your browser and download it as a transparent PNG — no account, no payment, no watermark. Works on any device.

What documents cannot be signed electronically?

Exceptions vary by jurisdiction but typically include wills, certain real estate deeds, court orders, and specific government forms. Standard business contracts, NDAs, invoices, and employment agreements are all valid with electronic signatures.

Do I need special software for electronic signatures?

No. Any device with a browser can create an electronic signature. Paid platforms add workflow features like document routing and tracking, but the signature itself can be created for free and inserted into any document manually.
